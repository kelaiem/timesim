One tooth of the escape wheel, magnified, worn over a century of watch life with a service every 5 years. At each service the watch is cleaned of its worn steel and old oil and re-oiled, so the wear rate stays the same from one interval to the next. The steel lost is too small to draw on the 3D movement: the worst case over the whole century is about 17 µm, less than the tooth's own edge bevel. So it is drawn here instead, at a scale where it shows. The wear is modelled, not simulated. The rate comes from Archard's wear law, using the loads and sliding distances the escapement calculation gives. The one number the movement cannot measure, the wear coefficient K, is a published range, shown as three cases.

The oil law, and why it is posed

Between services a watch oil spreads, thickens and oxidises, its friction rises, and the balance swings less. No source this model uses gives a measured curve for how that happens, so the page uses a posed law: one stated on purpose and labelled as such, not simulated.

Which friction ages comes from the movement's lubrication map: every friction figure that a lubricated joint is priced with. That is .

The two ends are the ends of the movement's own friction ranges: fresh oil is the nominal figure, the serviced watch; oil at the end of an interval is the worst figure the range allows, which the jewel row already describes as thinned or aged oil.

The shape between them is the posed part: a straight line in time since the last service. A real oil probably holds up and then fails late, which would keep the swing up for longer and drop it harder near the end.

What this does and does not claim

The worn shape is the model's, not a measured one. The wear takes a volume of steel off the tip. The page shows that volume removed as a flat cut across the tooth, square to the wheel's radius. That is the direction the lock's allowance is measured in: the pallet stones are set to the tooth circle, and a shorter tooth circle eats into the lock. A real tip would wear rounder. The depth is what matters for the lock, and the depth is what the model prices.

The allowance is how far the tooth can shorten before the lock is down to its safety share, the part kept in reserve for shocks. Only the extra "run" beyond that can be spent. Part of it is already spent before the watch runs: the tip is finished to a small radius, which sets it back from its sharp drawn point.

The oil's ageing is posed, and it moves only the swing. The swing chart is the movement's own energy calculation at the aged friction, but the way the friction climbs is a stated law, not a measured one. The aged oil does not feed back into the wear: the wear rate is the same all through each interval, because the wear coefficient's range is meant to cover a serviced, oiled contact. Nor does it move the rate the watch keeps: the record has no law for how the rate changes with the swing. The oil on the pallet faces, which in a real watch is the first to age, is not one of the frictions that age here, because the lubrication map prices no friction figure for it.

Not modelled here: worn particles that stay in the oil and speed up wear (assumed cleaned out at each service); wear on the pallet stones, which are much harder than the steel; and wear in the pivots. Time on this page is a slider, not the movement's clock.
