Gear teeth — cycloidal, and derived rather than drawnevery train · src/geometry.js §136

Until §136 every wheel and pinion in the movement was cut with straight-chord flanks and no pressure angle — correctly placed outlines rather than gears. Two of those rolling together have no conjugate action at all, which is why the power-reserve train's wheels were visibly passing through each other. The teeth are now CYCLOIDAL, which is what a watch of this kind uses and not an arbitrary choice: a cycloidal pair tolerates the varying centre distance and the very low leaf counts a watch train actually runs (this movement has a seven-leaf pinion), where an involute pinion of that count undercuts badly.

Four quantities are DERIVED here rather than chosen, each from a constraint that is written beside it in the source. The describing circle is m·min(mates)/4 — half the smallest mate's pitch radius, so the pinion it is most likely to be driving gets radial flanks. The two addenda come from a pair solve that targets a contact ratio above one, capped where the tip would come to a point. Backlash is a share of the pitch, 0.02·π·m + 2ε, and root clearance is 0.05 + ε. The tessellation budget ε is 0.01·π·m, set at half the phase instrument's guaranteed slack: the chords of a convex face lie INSIDE the metal, so tessellating errs on the clearance side and spends only backlash.

That the flanks genuinely roll is measured, not asserted. tools/probe-136-roll.mjs builds all twenty-four of the movement's gear meshes from the same generator the builders call, places each at its real centre distance and rolls it through a full tooth pitch at the conjugate ratio: twenty-four of twenty-four at zero penetration, with backlash left over.

What this does NOT yet claim. The teeth are MODELLED as conjugate geometry; the wheels are still POSED by tooth-count arithmetic rather than driven by contact, so nothing here transmits a force. And the shipped metal is not quite the metal that was cut: the extrusion adds a bevel by offsetting the outline outward, and that offset is larger than the backlash the generator reserved — so two mating teeth still overlap slightly in the built scene even though their profiles roll cleanly. TODO 84 carries it, with the reserve train's two rows waived against TODO 77 until it lands. The bound that measures how far past its tip circle a tooth reaches is over-generous for a related reason of its own (TODO 86). Two builders also still cut the old trapezoid: the barrel, because converting it moves its cavity and that is a re-solve of the equalisation work rather than a geometry change, and the differential's bevel wheels, because a sheared planar cycloid is not a bevel tooth. Mainspring — one length of steel, redistributedpower · src/geometry.js, src/main.js

A mainspring has two fixed ends. The inner one is hooked to a collar that stands still — it belongs to the set-up work on the plate side, not to the drum — and the outer one is riveted into the drum wall, which turns. Winding is therefore not a rotation of the spring: it is a change of the spring's shape. The same length of ribbon spans a different number of turns between the same two radii, bunching onto the arbor as it is wound and spreading toward the wall as it runs down. This movement has two springs built this way; the alarm's is the entry further down.

The model says exactly that. The drum's angle is the only input; the ribbon's whole geometry follows from it, as one of 34 precomputed wind frames. Two constraints pick the shape, and neither is a taste: no two turns may lie closer than the ribbon's own thickness (coils touch, they do not merge), and every frame must be the same length of steel, because steel does not grow.

Handedness was not chosen. The drum's rotation rises as the reserve falls, so the ribbon has to lose turns as the drum turns — which makes the spiral run clockwise outward. Wound the other way it would gain turns while it drove. Nothing could see that while the whole spiral simply rotated as a rigid body.

Fusee & chain — constant force from a dying springpower · src/main.js

The mainspring lives in a drum: a coiled ribbon hooked to the drum wall at its outer end (the hook is riveted into the wall — a declared joint the intra-unit instrument checks). As it unwinds, its torque falls — a spring near full wind pulls much harder than one near empty, and a train driven directly would beat measurably differently across the reserve.

The fusee is the correction: a cone with a spiral chain groove, chained to the drum. Fully wound, the chain pulls at the cone's small radius — high spring force, short lever. As the spring runs down the chain works toward the large radius — weaker force, longer lever. The product, torque into the train, stays near constant. Winding spools the chain back onto the fusee; a maintaining detent keeps the train driven while the wind briefly unloads it, so the escapement never runs backward.

Seen from the side, the whole bargain is one drawing. The chain leaves the drum's coil, crosses a span of open plate and climbs the cone's groove, and BOTH of its ends travel while it does: the cone's active groove rises as the wrap builds, and the drum's coil descends from its hook one chain thickness per turn. Plate 1 is that elevation, driven by the reserve — every wrap and every coil turn plotted from the parametric curve the sim cuts its links from.

So much for where the chain is. What it is for is the product of two numbers: the spring's torque, which falls as it unwinds, and the radius the chain pulls at, which grows. Multiply them and the train should feel nothing change. That claim was worth drawing rather than asserting, and drawing it is what caught the cone out — the taper that holds the product level is not a straight line, and this one used to be one. The flank is now the curve the equalisation asks for, so the plate that found the gap is also the plate that shows it shut. The ideal curve then failed a harder test — physically carrying its own chain (TODO 46) — and the fix was not to soften the law but to re-gear the first stage under it; the plate draws the cut that resulted.

One mechanism is left, and it is the one that makes a fusee usable. Winding turns the cone backwards against the train — so for as long as you are winding, the thing that drives the watch has let go of it. Harrison's answer is a sandwich at the great wheel: the wheel runs loose on the arbor, and drive reaches it through a second spring that is kept wound by the train itself. While the fusee reverses, that spring is what feeds the escapement, and a detent on the plate holds the wheel it pushes against.

Swiss lever escapement — the four contact phases of a beatregulation · src/main.js, src/geometry.js, src/layout.js

The balance and its hairspring are the oscillator; everything else exists to keep it swinging and to count its swings. Each beat is a fixed sequence of mechanical contacts, and the source builds every one by name (makePalletFork): the stone's locking corner solved onto the wheel's tooth circle, the impulse face cut as the plane through that corner, and draw ("LEAN") as a locking face angled so the wheel's own pressure pulls the fork deeper into its banking — with a draw-torque-sign assert, because a draw that pushes the wrong way unlocks itself.

Two arrows, and they run opposite ways. ENERGY travels wheel → stone → fork → roller pin → balance: the train pushes, and the escapement exists to hand a sip of that push to the oscillator twice a beat. POSITION travels the other way. The escape wheel is not a wheel that turns and is occasionally interrupted — it is a wheel that stands still, and moves only in the instants a stone lets it, exactly half a tooth pitch and no further, because the other stone is already waiting. Ask what moved the wheel and the answer, in every phase but one, is: the stones did. That is the reading the plate below is drawn in, and it is also the one this build computes — escapeAngle is a function of the balance's phase (plate 2), so in the code the stones do not merely gate the wheel, they are its clock.

"Swiss" names the DESIGN, not a provenance — and this build earns the name in geometry: the straight-line layout (escape centre, fork pivot, and balance centre measure exactly collinear — 180.0° at the fork, from solveLayout's own P table) and a 15-tooth CLUB-TOOTH wheel, the impulse shared between tooth face and stone. A German-styled movement carrying a Swiss lever is the industry's norm, Glashütte's included; the historical Glashütter Ankerhemmung is a distinct variant of the lever family, and not what is built here.

Ledger: the tick models the escapement KINEMATICALLY — the wheel's angle is a law of balance phase, which is the POSITION arrow and only that. The energy arrow is drawn here, not driven: no torque in this movement is dynamically INTEGRATED (TODO 32 derived the mainspring's torque law and the fusee delivers it level, but that is a published moment, not a force stepping this train), so the impulse of panel 3 transfers no energy, the amplitude it would sustain is a declared constant, and lift angles and safety-action dynamics are not simulated. The drawn contacts are the built geometry's and the motion law is the shipped one. Note what that leaves standing: the stones of a real escapement DO command the wheel's position — the plates above claim no more than that — and here they command it absolutely, because there is no torque to argue with. The pallet stones seat in slots whose gap is DERIVED from the blank's own extrude bevel (gGap = bevel + SEAT_SHOW) — a guard born from a defect every battery run missed; that seat is also TODO 5's fixture-vs-fixture residue: the one intra-unit pair class the interim instrument still cannot see. Banking: FORK_BANK_DEG = rollerR·Δθ_pin / notchDepth / 2, derived, with FORK_RECOIL_DEG at a quarter of it.

Free-sprung balance — regulation without a regulatorregulation · src/main.js ~4075–4165

There is deliberately no regulator on this balance: no index arm, no curb pins — the hairspring's terminal curve runs uninterrupted to its stud (the build's own comment: "FREE-SPRUNG: there is deliberately NO regulator"). On an indexed watch the curb pins pinch the spring to shorten its acting length; free-sprung watches delete that interference and put the rate in the balance's own inertia instead — the timing screws threaded into the rim. Screwing a pair in reduces the moment of inertia and the watch gains; out, it loses. The spring is never touched.

Zero reset — the heart cam's one low pointdisplay · src/main.js ~1281

The seconds display can be snapped back to zero without stopping the train — the stopwatch architecture, applied to the small seconds. The display rides a slip-coupled arbor (a friction fit on the fourth wheel's axis), and that arbor carries a heart cam: a spiral whose radius falls continuously to a single lowest point. On reset, a spring-driven hammer falls against the heart's edge; wherever the cam happens to be, the pressure on a falling-radius spiral produces torque toward the low point, so the heart rotates until the hammer's pad seats in the notch — and the low point is cut at the display's zero. The train never feels it: the slip coupling pays for the jump.

Hacking seconds — a brake that respects the timing screwsregulation · src/main.js ~1275, 2431–2447

Pull the crown to set the time and the balance stops dead — hacking — so the watch can be synchronised to a reference. The brake is the stop work: the setting chain's motion travels a thin hack rod to a crank beside the balance, and the crank's ruby pad rises against the flat underside of the balance rim. Push the crown home and the pad falls away; the hairspring restarts the balance itself.

Which surface, and why it is not the obvious one. The rim's outer edge is where a brake would naturally press, and that is the one surface it cannot have: the timing screws are radial, and they stand proud of that edge all the way round. They do not stand proud of either flat face — the screw stock is thinner than the rim is tall, so the screws lie inside the rim's z-band — which is exactly why the underside is free. The pad presses that underside on a flat annulus starting at the rim's inner edge. It is not the rim's inner wall, and it is not a gap between two screws: the rim swings under a station that stays put, so the clearance has to hold at every angle of the balance at once.

Motion works & minute quick-set — 12:1 by tooth counts, minutes that stepdisplay · src/main.js

The hands never receive a computed angle. The cannon pinion turns with the train; the minute wheel and its pinion gear it down to the hour wheel, and the 12:1 arrives because the tooth counts multiply to it — the repo's rule 2. Hand-setting drives the same wheels forward from the crown through the keyless chain, so a set hand and a driven hand are the same hand.

Minute setting here is a quick-set with a detented display — deliberately not the words "jumping minute", which would claim more than this is. A star wheel, a sprung beak (the jumper) and a click spring engage only while the crown is OUT, so a running watch's minute hand creeps as it always did; pull the crown and the hand parks between graduations and steps one whole minute at a time. The beak's clearance over the star (0.1500, exactly one CLEAR_MARGIN) is the solved release gap, and the step is an ease the sim's setPose deliberately cannot fake through.

Keyless works — one crown, two jobsinterface · src/main.js

The crown's stem carries a sliding pinion on a square: pushed home, the clutch routes the crown's turn into the winding path and the chain spools back onto the fusee. Pulled out, the sliding pinion drops into the setting wheels and the same turn walks the motion works instead. The clutch's travel is eased in the sim (a pulled crown is a motion, not a flag), and every mesh along both paths has its phase solved from measurement rather than assumed (§15's closing work).

The stem's one-way is metal. The first build's sliding pinion was two parts pretending to be one — it slid with the stem AND meshed the crown wheel, and the backward free-wheel every crown owner has felt was a bookkeeping scalar. The split gives the stem its real anatomy: a fixed WINDING PINION that always meshes the crown wheel and turns with the banked wind, and the sliding CLUTCH keyed to the stem's square, the two joined by a Breguet-style saw coupling. Winding, the drive faces bear; turned backward, the ramps cam the clutch axially over the pinion's ring, one snap per leaf, and the yoke's spring — real metal on its own post — reseats it. One profile law cuts both rings, rides the pose and answers the battery, so the metal and its law cannot disagree; the tooth is cut narrower than its socket on purpose, and that backlash is why the faces bear while the ramps hold daylight.

The stem rides a bushing block, the setting wheel seats against that bushing's boss — all of it declared joints in the intra-unit instrument's table, because a pivot in its bearing is what an assembly is; the instrument exists for parts that foul, not parts that join.

The column wheel — a rotary switch with teethalarm · src/main.js §43, §25 D

The alarm's on/off switch is a chronograph's column wheel: a castellated crown of six columns over a saw-toothed ratchet skirt. Each press of the case pusher drives a pawl one saw tooth — half a column pitch — so every actuation flips the state: a rider standing on a column is held out (alarm OFF); over a gap it drops in (ON). Two members read the same castellations at whole pitches apart, so they always agree: the lock lever's beak (gates the stop) and the §35 link beak (arms the ring). What HOLDS the wheel where a press leaves it reads something else entirely — §173 moved the index onto the saw, where a sautoir seats between two teeth.

Alarm arming — one press, thirty units of movementalarm · src/main.js §35

The column wheel lives out near the rim; the selector ring that actually arms the alarm lives at the centre — a span no lever should cross. The real-watch answer is a lay shaft: the §35 link beak reads the castellations (same parity as the lock beak — §68 re-sited the rod diametrically opposite it, three whole pitches, so parity holds by construction), and its tail (≈7:1 to the beak — the built arms measure 9.95/1.395, TODO 63's re-take; the 26.79 tail §35 grew to reach a corridor-fixed rod is retired) drives a vertical rod down through bores in both plates, then a rim crank, the radial shaft, and a centre crank pressing the ring's drive tab. What holds it there is a SAUTOIR on the saw, and §173 moved it off the castellations for reasons that are structural rather than stylistic. A part rode the columns until then, and it could not detent the wheel in any state: the wheel stops twelve times a revolution and there are six columns, and worse, a column's top and a gap's floor are both arcs of CONSTANT radius — the chamfer is cut in z only — so the follower was pushed out by nothing at every stop it actually used. The saw already has one tooth per index, and a tooth space is a V.

Alarm train — set, wound, struck, silencedalarm · src/main.js

Setting: the alarm crown turns a bevel pair onto a friction-set disc under the dial (the Vulcain/Memovox way — it holds wherever the crown leaves it, read to the nearest quarter mark), while a central alarm hand rides its own tube through the hand stack, styled as a steel shadow of the hour hand so parked it disappears under it. The arming run — pusher → pawl → column wheel → ring — is DRIVEN from its input rather than posed from its output: the press carries the pawl, the pawl indexes the wheel a whole tooth, and every member downstream stands where the surface driving it puts it — each hand-off measured shut at both parities on every battery run by alarmHandoffs. The only rate left in the chain is how fast a finger presses. What it does not claim is dynamics: no force, friction or spring rate is integrated anywhere in it. Alarm barrel — the second spring, wound at its arboralarm · src/main.js §89 §99, src/geometry.js

The alarm runs on its own mainspring, and until §89 that spring was not one. Body, arbor and ribbon were a single rotating member, so the coil turned with the drum it sits in and its shape never changed between a full wind and a dead barrel. A ribbon with both ends on the same part cannot store anything: winding is the relative angle between two members, and there was only one.

Since §112 the whole power side of this mechanism — barrel, winding tier, ratchet and click, governor and its anchor — lives UNDER the three-quarter plate, grounded in the base plate, with only the strike group (cam, hammer, gong, lock, switch) left on the top face and the strike arbor passing through a plate bore. Since §99 it is the textbook alarm barrel: a true going barrel that winds its arbor. The winding train — 12 into 44 through two idlers, the same ratio the rim-wound era had, because the arbor's winding wheel deliberately takes the rim's own tooth count — lands on a wheel keyed to the arbor; a saw-toothed ratchet beside it is held by a plate-grounded click, and the body delivers to the striking train from its rim. Winding turns the arbor over a parked body (the striker stands through a wind); ringing runs the body under a parked arbor — the click's hold, one mesh from the spring it is holding. The hook-shaped click is a measured necessity, not a style: a straight blade at this tooth pitch fouls an adjacent tooth at every park, so the arm stays outside the tooth tips and a beak cut to the tooth space itself dips in — its leading edge parallel to the tooth face it holds against, its back relieved along the ramp it rides while winding, its seat re-solved every tick against the saw the teeth were actually cut from. When the hand leaves the crown, the loaded arbor gives back the parked fraction of one pitch — at most 1/32 of a turn — until that face meets the beak, and the recoil travels the gears: the winding train and the crown snap back a hair, as a real barrel's do.

The body turns on the arbor, so floor and lid are bored for it — 0.644 against the arbor's 0.594, which is PIVOT_BORE_CLEAR 0.05, the same running clearance every other journal in this movement is cut with. That bore is not decoration: before the arbor stood still, both faces passed straight through it and no instrument could object, because a group's own parts were all fixtures together. The moment one member stops moving, the other's floor is a mover against it.

The hook the inner end butts against is placed by derivation, not by eye. The end sits at a constant azimuth in the movement frame — that is the anchoring claim — and the builder reports it, so the lug is set from that angle, one ribbon thickness wide, offset half its width onto the side the spring pushes from. Measured across the wind, the inner end holds its anchor to within half a frame step while the outer end sweeps the full 1.75 turns with the body.

Winding arrest — the stop that makes overwinding impossiblegoing · src/main.js §126

A fusee cannot slip. The chain is a fixed length hooked at both ends, so when the last coil is home there is nowhere for another turn to go — and until this mechanism existed, the sim said so with a number instead of a part: the bank was clamped, and past full wind the crown, crown wheel and transfer wheel kept turning against a frozen winding spur, sliding teeth through teeth. Now a part is in the way. The arriving last coil slides under a sprung finger and lifts it; the lifted finger's beak drops in front of a lug standing on the cone and turning with it; beak meets lug, and the whole path from the lug back to the hand stops together. The clamp is gone from the winding branch entirely — the cap is a consequence of that contact.

The engagement turn count is not chosen. It IS FUSEE_WRAP_TURNS — the chain's engagement is f = tension·FUSEE_F_ACTIVE, so the last link arrives exactly at full wrap — and the identity is asserted at boot against the energy side's RESERVE_BARREL_TURNS. That the lug can be CUT to that angle rather than calibrated to it rests on a second fact: the barrel drains at exactly the rate the train turns the cone (one turn per HOURS_PER_FUSEE_TURN), so the cone's world angle is a pure function of the banked reserve and full wind is one fixed azimuth forever. Before this landing those two rates disagreed by 2.14× — the reserve readout promised 30 h and the movement ran about 14 — and no fixed stop could have existed at all.

The finger's working face rides the wall it meets: it leans with the wrap's own §124 tilt, and the law that poses it reads the DISCRETE links the mesh lays, because a chain plate's outer edge chords between its pins and a contact measured to a hundredth cannot ride a smooth curve that ignores that. The beak's face is a radial plane with a TANGENTIAL normal, which is the whole mechanics of an arrest: a tangential reaction is the only kind that can stop a rotating cone. Cut perpendicular to the pivot line instead — as this first was — and the contact exerts no torque on the cone whatever; it slides past, with every collision gate green. The arm runs tangentially so the lug's push is compression into the pivot, and what moment survives is held in the ENGAGING sense.

Fitting the finger around its neighbours turned out to be the harder half, and it is solved WITH the finger rather than after it. Every law that says where the chain is now reads the same discrete links the pad's law does — a stop derived from the smooth stations put the beak's arm inside the coil while every station-based assert read clear — and each arm is held along its whole chord, over the throw the lift law actually performs rather than the one it was designed for. The arms stop outside the wrap and the tabs bridge inward from there, because an arm spans thirty degrees of a cone that is climbing and a tab spans three. The last piece was the lug: giving it the chain's own proudness had been a tidy tie to nothing, and it left the finger's pivot with no legal position at all — it must stand clear of the wrap, yet a beak lever of the designed ratio that presses itself INTO its stop rather than out of it can only reach so far inward. The lug is cut as proud as that requirement demands now, never less proud than the coil whose place it takes.

Open debt, stated rather than hidden. The chain's FUSEE end is hooked to nothing: the drum end has a real claw, the cone end has no metal behind the support edge that claims one, and the wrap simply starts wherever the tangent puts it. The finger's own debt is paid: its lift law now samples the BUILT chain — the same stamped buffer the display swaps in, each link at its own parity, every wrapped link in its window — and the arm swings by the lever's exact inverse rather than a first-order gain, so the pad rides the coil with measured contact through the arming band and the full-wind kiss holds inside its ±0.03 band, the waiver retired. The entry's other two debts are paid too: the going stem's one-way is modelled metal now — the saw coupling at the sliding clutch, told in the keyless works entry — and the plate's own floor law counts the chain's top coil among the parts it must clear — the one under-plate occupant no measured box had ever named — so the gap over the coil holds the 0.15 margin at full wind, asserted at every boot instead of filed as debt.

Alarm stop-work — a differential counts the windalarm · src/main.js §106 §129

The alarm barrel is a plain barrel, not a fusee, and a plain multi-turn barrel is the classical home of Maltese stop-work. The going side's arrest stops the hand when the chain runs out of chain; this side has no chain to run out, so something has to COUNT. A finger with one pin indexes a crossed wheel one station per turn; after seven of them the cross presents an arm with no slot in it, the pin butts against that arm, and the whole winding path stops together. Until this existed the cap was a number in the tick law, and the crown kept turning against a frozen train.

But what has to be counted is the WIND, and that is harder than it sounds. The wind is the angle the ribbon holds BETWEEN the barrel's arbor and its body, and neither member alone carries it. While the alarm is wound the click parks the body, so the arbor's angle is the wind; while it rings the click parks the ARBOR and lets the body run, so the same angle says nothing at all. A stop-work geared to either member is a stop-work that works once: the first build here was geared to the arbor, and measured, its cross stood still through an entire run-down and the second wind drove the pin straight through the blank arm.

Reading a difference needs a part with two degrees of freedom, and WHICH part is arithmetic rather than taste. Every three-port epicyclic obeys out = α·in1 + (1−α)·in2 — the coefficients sum to one whatever the tooth counts — so two inputs of equal size subtract only at α = ½ exactly. That is a SPIDER: two equal bevels facing each other from a shared apex with planets between them, its cage turning at the plain mean of the pair. A sun-planet-annulus cannot reach it, because it would need annulus teeth equal to sun teeth. This is also why the differential in a real power-reserve indicator is a spider.

So one leg takes the arbor's own wheel direct and the other takes the body's rim through an idler, which reverses it — the idler's two counts are equal, so they cancel out of the ratio entirely and its whole duty is that sign. The spider halves their sum, an output stage doubles it back, and what leaves the cage is the wind and neither member: turn both together and the finger does not move at all. Wind the alarm and the cross walks forward; let it ring and the same finger walks it back, so the stop-work resets as the spring empties.

Nothing about the count is chosen. Full wind is ALARM_BARREL_TURNS turns against a barrel of ALARM_WIND_W teeth, and the chain is geared so the finger turns four times per turn of wind: 4 × 1.75 = 7. A single-pin Geneva travels N−1 turns, which fixes ARREST_STATIONS at eight: the textbook cross, one finger, one pin, seven slots and the blank arm. The stop lands on ARREST_CLICKS clicks of the arbor ratchet because the train adds no quantisation of its own.

The relation that sizes it is easy to get backwards. At entry the pin's velocity is perpendicular to the crank and must lie along the slot, which is radial on the cross — so the right angle is AT THE PIN, and the crank radius is the side opposite the half-index. The driver's pin circle is the SMALL radius and the cross the large one. Swapping them still satisfies a² + b² = d², which is why an inverted pair survives an eyeball check; what catches it is the ANGLE, and this build asserts that the index measures 2π/N rather than trusting it. Built inverted, the index comes out 135° — 2.667 stations, not an integer at all.

Three floors set the cross's size, and the obvious two are not the binding one. The rim pitch must carry a slot and two walls; the web between the hub and the slot bottoms must hold the arms on; and mid-engagement the cross's rim runs right past the driver's centre, so the arbor the finger is keyed to has to fit through the gap. The nearest metal there is not the slot's centreline — that is the gap the pin rides in — but the HORN beside it, and what it must clear is the finger's bore lip, not the bare arbor. That third floor is what governs, and genevaSpec solves it by bisection because the horn's angle depends on the radius it is setting.

The station is solved, not placed. The pinion's centre is not free — it must sit on the mesh circle or it does not mesh — so the freedoms are its azimuth on that circle, the finger and cross's plane, and the cross's azimuth about its arbor. The build sweeps all three and scores the assembly as PIECES, each at its own radius: the pinion, the finger's full pin sweep, the cross's rim, and the two COLUMNS that run from the plate up to their part and so are judged over every band they cross. Four things it learned the hard way are written into it — that a mesh partner is not an obstacle to the wheel that meshes it, that the columns are pieces, that the low corridor's swept footprint is declared rather than visible, and that a rotor's footprint is its annulus and not its resting silhouette.

Two things size the differential, and both were got wrong first. A cone's rim stands one tip radius OFF the cage's axis as well as one out along its own, so what a planet SWEEPS is √2 times its tip — size it to the tip and the planets reach half again as far as anything was told. And a TOOTH is a section like any other, so the movement's own metal floor reaches it: a standard tooth is π·module/2 thick at the pitch line. Without that floor, a part asked to fit a space nothing fits will shrink its teeth until it does, and report success.

Open debt, stated rather than hidden. No pose axis reverses the wind within a single sweep — one runs it up, another runs it down, neither turns round — so the no-spring audit still cannot judge the cross, and no restoring element is declared for it. The reversal is real now, which makes that axis more owed than it was when the mechanism did not have one. The build-time siting solve remains a cheap gate rather than the acceptance: it scores the scene at rest and only against the parts built before it, and three of its obstacle classes were added only after the battery found what it had missed. And the demonstration half — a script verb that winds the alarm, and a readout that can say “arrested” rather than a percentage — is filed, not built.

A wound spring does not know how fast to ring: ungoverned, a striking train accelerates until something velocity-dependent eats the surplus, so every real striking work carries a speed limiter chosen on purpose. This movement's is the alarm-clock answer, picked by arithmetic over its rivals — a fly (air vane) is refuted at watch scale (balancing the working torque at these speeds demands 40–50 mm vanes, clock metal an order past the whole movement), and the repeater's centrifugal friction governor is real but costs an ~800:1 train whirring at ~125 rev/s. The gong's voice — partials from the bar, not a chosen notealarm · src/main.js §56

The alarm's sound is not a sample and not a picked frequency: the gong is a clamped-free steel bar, and its voice is derived from that bar's own bending modes. A clamped-free bar's overtones are fixed by physics, not tuning: the second mode rings at 6.27× the fundamental — that is (k₂L/k₁L)² = (4.694/1.875)² from the beam equation, and it is why struck gongs sound metallic rather than musical (the partials are inharmonic). The build states it as its own comment: "the wire's OWN modes, not a chosen note. The 2nd sits at 6.27× the [fundamental]".
