Mainspring — one length of steel, redistributedpower · src/geometry.js, src/main.js

A mainspring has two fixed ends. The inner one is hooked to a collar that stands still — it belongs to the set-up work on the plate side, not to the drum — and the outer one is riveted into the drum wall, which turns. Winding is therefore not a rotation of the spring: it is a change of the spring's shape. The same length of ribbon spans a different number of turns between the same two radii, bunching onto the arbor as it is wound and spreading toward the wall as it runs down. This movement has two springs built this way; the alarm's is the entry further down.

The model says exactly that. The drum's angle is the only input; the ribbon's whole geometry follows from it, as one of 88 precomputed wind frames. Two constraints pick the shape, and neither is a taste: no two turns may lie closer than the ribbon's own thickness (coils touch, they do not merge), and every frame must be the same length of steel, because steel does not grow.

Handedness was not chosen. The drum's rotation rises as the reserve falls, so the ribbon has to lose turns as the drum turns — which makes the spiral run clockwise outward. Wound the other way it would gain turns while it drove. Nothing could see that while the whole spiral simply rotated as a rigid body.

Honesty ledger: TODO 1 is closed — this shape is derived, not posed, and the arbor hook is a stub one ribbon thickness proud of the collar because at full wind the second coil comes down to bind and anything longer would be buried in it. TODO 32 is open: the spring's torque is still authored (springTq = 0.35 + 0.65 * reserveShown in the HUD), and the fusee cone's taper is derived from that curve rather than from this ribbon. The shape is simulated; the force is not. TODO 39 is open: the parts the ribbon's inner end is pinned to — the collar and its hook — are static, which is what makes the wind real, but the arbor cylinder they sit on is still parented to the drum and turns with it. Nothing can see it (a cylinder spinning about its own axis looks identical either way) and nothing measures it, which is exactly why it is written down here. §89 built the option that fixes it, on the alarm barrel.

Fusee & chain — constant force from a dying springpower · src/main.js

The mainspring lives in a drum: a coiled ribbon hooked to the drum wall at its outer end (the hook is riveted into the wall — a declared joint the intra-unit instrument checks). As it unwinds, its torque falls — a spring near full wind pulls much harder than one near empty, and a train driven directly would beat measurably differently across the reserve.

The fusee is the correction: a cone with a spiral chain groove, chained to the drum. Fully wound, the chain pulls at the cone's small radius — high spring force, short lever. As the spring runs down the chain works toward the large radius — weaker force, longer lever. The product, torque into the train, stays near constant. Winding spools the chain back onto the fusee; a maintaining detent keeps the train driven while the wind briefly unloads it, so the escapement never runs backward.

Seen from the side, the whole bargain is one drawing. The chain leaves the drum's coil, crosses a span of open plate and climbs the cone's groove, and BOTH of its ends travel while it does: the cone's active groove rises as the wrap builds, and the drum's coil descends from its hook one chain thickness per turn. Plate 1 is that elevation, driven by the reserve — every wrap and every coil turn plotted from the parametric curve the sim cuts its links from.

So much for where the chain is. What it is for is the product of two numbers: the spring's torque, which falls as it unwinds, and the radius the chain pulls at, which grows. Multiply them and the train should feel nothing change. That is the claim, and it is worth drawing rather than asserting — because the taper that would actually hold the product level is not a straight line.

One mechanism is left, and it is the one that makes a fusee usable. Winding turns the cone backwards against the train — so for as long as you are winding, the thing that drives the watch has let go of it. Harrison's answer is a sandwich at the great wheel: the wheel runs loose on the arbor, and drive reaches it through a second spring that is kept wound by the train itself. While the fusee reverses, that spring is what feeds the escapement, and a detent on the plate holds the wheel it pushes against.

Honesty ledger: the equalisation is the open item here. TODO 40 (new, from plate 2) files three arithmetic gaps in one place — the straight generator cannot level a linear spring's product, the HUD multiplies by a radius the chain never reaches, and CHAIN_ENGAGED books the wind at FUSEE_AVG_R 5.0 when the wrap's own mean radius is 5.15, so the drum turns about 3% short of the chain the cone takes up. TODO 32 is upstream of all of it: the spring's torque law is authored rather than emerging from ribbon mechanics, and the cone's taper was derived from that curve (TODO 1, the ribbon's geometry, is closed — see the mainspring entry). The maintaining sandwich is P0-clean by comparison: pawls, detent and spring are real meshes on real pivots, driven from the fusee's own reverse rotation. The chain's swept fan is a first-class obstacle in the layout (§36's registry).

Swiss lever escapement — the four contact phases of a beatregulation · src/main.js, src/geometry.js, src/layout.js

The balance and its hairspring are the oscillator; everything else exists to keep it swinging and to count its swings. Each beat is a fixed sequence of mechanical contacts, and the source builds every one by name (makePalletFork): the stone's locking corner solved onto the wheel's tooth circle, the impulse face cut as the plane through that corner, and draw ("LEAN") as a locking face angled so the wheel's own pressure pulls the fork deeper into its banking — with a draw-torque-sign assert, because a draw that pushes the wrong way unlocks itself.

Two arrows, and they run opposite ways. ENERGY travels wheel → stone → fork → roller pin → balance: the train pushes, and the escapement exists to hand a sip of that push to the oscillator twice a beat. POSITION travels the other way. The escape wheel is not a wheel that turns and is occasionally interrupted — it is a wheel that stands still, and moves only in the instants a stone lets it, exactly half a tooth pitch and no further, because the other stone is already waiting. Ask what moved the wheel and the answer, in every phase but one, is: the stones did. That is the reading the plate below is drawn in, and it is also the one this build computes — escapeAngle is a function of the balance's phase (plate 2), so in the code the stones do not merely gate the wheel, they are its clock.

"Swiss" names the DESIGN, not a provenance — and this build earns the name in geometry: the straight-line layout (escape centre, fork pivot, and balance centre measure exactly collinear — 180.0° at the fork, from solveLayout's own P table) and a 15-tooth CLUB-TOOTH wheel, the impulse shared between tooth face and stone. A German-styled movement carrying a Swiss lever is the industry's norm, Glashütte's included; the historical Glashütter Ankerhemmung is a distinct variant of the lever family, and not what is built here.

Ledger: the tick models the escapement KINEMATICALLY — the wheel's angle is a law of balance phase, which is the POSITION arrow and only that. The energy arrow is drawn here, not driven: nothing in this movement makes a torque yet (TODO 32 — the mainspring's shape is real, its torque is one authored line), so the impulse of panel 3 transfers no energy, the amplitude it would sustain is a declared constant, and lift angles and safety-action dynamics are not simulated. The drawn contacts are the built geometry's and the motion law is the shipped one. Note what that leaves standing: the stones of a real escapement DO command the wheel's position — the plates above claim no more than that — and here they command it absolutely, because there is no torque to argue with. The pallet stones seat in slots whose gap is DERIVED from the arm's own extrude bevel (gGap = armBevel + SEAT_SHOW) — a guard born from a defect every battery run missed; that seat is also TODO 5's fixture-vs-fixture residue: the one intra-unit pair class the interim instrument still cannot see. Banking: FORK_BANK_DEG = rollerR·Δθ_pin / notchDepth / 2, derived, with FORK_RECOIL_DEG at a quarter of it.

Free-sprung balance — regulation without a regulatorregulation · src/main.js ~4075–4165

There is deliberately no regulator on this balance: no index arm, no curb pins — the hairspring's terminal curve runs uninterrupted to its stud (the build's own comment: "FREE-SPRUNG: there is deliberately NO regulator"). On an indexed watch the curb pins pinch the spring to shorten its acting length; free-sprung watches delete that interference and put the rate in the balance's own inertia instead — the timing screws threaded into the rim. Screwing a pair in reduces the moment of inertia and the watch gains; out, it loses. The spring is never touched.

Zero reset — the heart cam's one low pointdisplay · src/main.js ~1281

The seconds display can be snapped back to zero without stopping the train — the stopwatch architecture, applied to the small seconds. The display rides a slip-coupled arbor (a friction fit on the fourth wheel's axis), and that arbor carries a heart cam: a spiral whose radius falls continuously to a single lowest point. On reset, a spring-driven hammer falls against the heart's edge; wherever the cam happens to be, the pressure on a falling-radius spiral produces torque toward the low point, so the heart rotates until the hammer's pad seats in the notch — and the low point is cut at the display's zero. The train never feels it: the slip coupling pays for the jump.

Hacking seconds — a brake that respects the timing screwsregulation · src/main.js ~1275, 2431–2447

Pull the crown to set the time and the balance stops dead — hacking — so the watch can be synchronised to a reference. The brake is the stop work: the setting chain's motion travels a thin hack rod to a crank beside the balance, and the crank's pad rises against the balance rim. Push the crown home and the pad falls away; the hairspring restarts the balance itself.

Motion works & minute jumper — 12:1 by tooth counts, minutes that snapdisplay · src/main.js

The hands never receive a computed angle. The cannon pinion turns with the train; the minute wheel and its pinion gear it down to the hour wheel, and the 12:1 arrives because the tooth counts multiply to it — the repo's rule 2. Hand-setting drives the same wheels forward from the crown through the keyless chain, so a set hand and a driven hand are the same hand.

The minute display is a jumping minute: a star wheel, a sprung beak (the jumper), and a click spring hold the minute hand parked between graduations, then snap it one step at the minute — the beak's clearance over the star (0.1500, exactly one CLEAR_MARGIN) is the solved release gap, and the snap is an ease the sim's setPose deliberately cannot fake through.

Honesty ledger: TODO 21 is CLOSED, and it is worth stating what it was. These two planes used to be stacked upside down — the chain landed on the minute wheel and hung the hour wheel behind it, so the hour tube climbed to the hands straight through the minute wheel's and star's tooth bands: 568 vertices standing inside the tube's wall, 0.225 deep, at every pose, with the first mesh above happening THROUGH the wall. No radius fixes that, because the teeth reach nearer the axis than any tube is thin; the defect was the stacking order. The hour wheel is dial-most now, as in a watch, and its tube crosses nothing that is not coaxial with it. Measured after: 0. Found by the per-contact floors instrument, and its waiver is deleted rather than renewed.

Keyless works — one crown, two jobsinterface · src/main.js

The crown's stem carries a sliding pinion on a square: pushed home, the clutch routes the crown's turn into the winding path — ratchet and click onto the drum, spooling the chain back onto the fusee. Pulled out, the sliding pinion drops into the setting wheels and the same turn walks the motion works instead. The clutch's travel is eased in the sim (a pulled crown is a motion, not a flag), and every mesh along both paths has its phase solved from measurement rather than assumed (§15's closing work).

The stem rides a bushing block, the crown collar seats on a bushing torus — all of it declared joints in the intra-unit instrument's table, because a pivot in its bearing is what an assembly is; the instrument exists for parts that foul, not parts that join.

The column wheel — a rotary switch with teethalarm · src/main.js §43, §25 D

The alarm's on/off switch is a chronograph's column wheel: a castellated crown of six columns over a saw-toothed ratchet skirt. Each press of the case pusher drives a pawl one saw tooth — half a column pitch — so every actuation flips the state: a rider standing on a column is held out (alarm OFF); over a gap it drops in (ON). Three members read the same castellations at whole pitches apart, so they always agree: the lock lever's beak (gates the brake), the sprung click (indexes the two stable states and makes the toggle read), and the §35 link beak (arms the ring).

Alarm arming — one press, thirty units of movementalarm · src/main.js §35

The column wheel lives out near the rim; the selector ring that actually arms the alarm lives at the centre — a span no lever should cross. The real-watch answer is a lay shaft: the §35 link beak reads the castellations (same parity as the lock beak — §68 re-sited the rod diametrically opposite it, three whole pitches, so parity holds by construction), and its tail (~3:1 to the beak since §68 — the 26.79 tail §35 grew to reach a corridor-fixed rod is retired) drives a vertical rod down through bores in both plates, then a rim crank, the radial shaft, and a centre crank pressing the ring's drive tab. Rod down = ring down = armed; alarmOn is a readout of the geometry, not a flag.

Arming shows the alarm hand — a biconditional, assertedalarm · src/main.js §45 stages 0/1

The alarm hand is only present when it means something: Hidden ⟺ ¬Armed ∧ ¬Setting. Armed, the selector ring's parity holds the hand's tube up; setting, the alarm crown's collar lifts it through the release sleeve so the owner can see what they are aiming. The spec is a biconditional because the implications alone would pass a hand left permanently visible — and it is asserted at boot at all four corners, evaluated on the mechanism's own laws (the ring parity from the §35 registration solve, the sleeve's cone cap from the collar's two plateaus).

Alarm release feeler — reading the set time by touchalarm · src/main.js:6433

The fixed reader of the alarm train, in the Vulcain Cricket / JLC Memovox pattern. A spring-biased lever rides a raised track on the alarm disc; the disc turns with the difference between the hour wheel and the set time, so its one notch arrives under the lever's pin exactly at coincidence. The pin falls, the tail rises to a banked stop, and the withdrawal of the climb pawl frees the strike train. No boolean anywhere: causality enters at the crown and arrives at the gong through closed contacts (the repo's P0 rule), and each hand-off below is a measured row of the alarmHandoffs battery.

Alarm train — set, wound, struck, silencedalarm · src/main.js

Setting: the alarm crown turns a bevel pair onto a friction-set disc under the dial (the Vulcain/Memovox way — it holds wherever the crown leaves it, read to the nearest quarter mark), while a central alarm hand rides its own tube through the hand stack, styled as a steel shadow of the hour hand so parked it disappears under it. Winding: the same crown pushed home spools the alarm barrel through its own train. Striking: when the feeler trips (full entry above), the alarm barrel drives the striking wheel, and the hammer — its fall a real sprung member, not a spring law (§48) — beats the gong; the ring runs down as the barrel empties, or stops early if the crown is pulled (§45's arrest, plate 4 above).

The arming run — pusher → pawl → column wheel → ring — is DRIVEN from its input rather than posed from its output: the press carries the pawl, the pawl indexes the wheel a whole tooth, and every member downstream stands where the surface driving it puts it — each hand-off measured shut at both parities on every battery run by alarmHandoffs. The only rate left in the chain is how fast a finger presses. What it does not claim is dynamics: no force, friction or spring rate is integrated anywhere in it, and TODO 31 records the debt that follows — the lock the column lifts has no return spring, so the column alone decides where it sits.

Alarm barrel — the second spring, wound at its teethalarm · src/main.js §89, src/geometry.js

The alarm runs on its own mainspring, and until §89 that spring was not one. Body, arbor and ribbon were a single rotating member, so the coil turned with the drum it sits in and its shape never changed between a full wind and a dead barrel. A ribbon with both ends on the same part cannot store anything: winding is the relative angle between two members, and there was only one.

It is the going drum's arrangement now, not a textbook alarm barrel's. The arbor is planted in the frame and the body is wound at its teeth — the drum is wound at its wall by the fusee chain, this one at its rim by the winding train, 12 into 44 through two idlers — so the ribbon's inner end is pinned to the movement while its outer end rides the body. Nothing moved to buy that: same station, same height, same two meshes. The other split, a wound arbor with a click on it, re-sites the winding train's last mesh and is filed as layout work rather than done here.

The body turns on the arbor, so floor and lid are bored for it — 0.644 against the arbor's 0.594, which is PIVOT_BORE_CLEAR 0.05, the same running clearance every other journal in this movement is cut with. That bore is not decoration: before the arbor stood still, both faces passed straight through it and no instrument could object, because a group's own parts were all fixtures together. The moment one member stops moving, the other's floor is a mover against it.

The hook the inner end butts against is placed by derivation, not by eye. The end sits at a constant azimuth in the movement frame — that is the anchoring claim — and the builder reports it, so the lug is set from that angle, one ribbon thickness wide, offset half its width onto the side the spring pushes from. Measured across the wind, the inner end holds its anchor to within half a frame step while the outer end sweeps the full 1.75 turns with the body.

Honesty ledger: the shape is simulated — 61 wind frames, each solved so the developed length never moves (4.8e-13 across all of them) and no two turns lie closer than the ribbon's own thickness. The force is not, exactly as TODO 32 says of the going spring: the ring's cadence is ALARM_STRIKE_GAP 0.42 s, a literal, and nothing derives it from this ribbon's section. TODO 37 is open: the barrel holds its wind through the striking wheel's lock four meshes downstream, because a fixed-arbor barrel wound at its rim has nowhere to put a click. TODO 38 is open: no inspector axis winds it — the sweeps run this barrel down and never back up, so one direction of a part that goes both ways is uncovered.

The gong's voice — partials from the bar, not a chosen notealarm · src/main.js §56

The alarm's sound is not a sample and not a picked frequency: the gong is a clamped-free steel bar, and its voice is derived from that bar's own bending modes. A clamped-free bar's overtones are fixed by physics, not tuning: the second mode rings at 6.27× the fundamental — that is (k₂L/k₁L)² = (4.694/1.875)² from the beam equation, and it is why struck gongs sound metallic rather than musical (the partials are inharmonic). The build states it as its own comment: "the wire's OWN modes, not a chosen note. The 2nd sits at 6.27× the [fundamental]".
