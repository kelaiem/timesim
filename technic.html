The technic fork

The movement's going, motion and reserve trains re-toothed to LEGO Technic's module-1 catalogue. Each chain below multiplies EXACTLY to the ratio the steel train serves, and each shaft turns by the product of the tooth counts before it.

Tooth counts, kinds, centre distances and ratios come from the declared rows. The build assembles every row from the real parts (LDraw.org's library, CC BY 4.0) on a brick frame whose every dimension is measured off those parts. The rows use no turntable: neither could be mounted on the frame, so the solver answers with spur gears on the stud lattice. The escapement and its balance are the eighth cell, below.

Beside the trains is the keyless works: the stem carries two 16T clutch gears and a driving ring that slides between them, LEGO's own changeover standing in for the steel sliding pinion. wind, set and neutral are the PULL: a rack under the selector's 28T pinion, pushed in to wind and drawn out to set, turns the selector on its axle over the stem, and its changeover catch carries the ring in its groove onto the clutch gear's web (the stem stops first, as the ring's lugs need it). A DETENT holds each mode: a half bush on a thin liftarm, banded onto a small gear on the selector axle (a 12T classic, a 14T 2026), rests in one of its gaps, and a mode is one tooth. Each branch's gears are the solver's answer from the clutch gear to the steel ratio. The rests are measured off the parts; the band is drawn posed, its force not measured.

The motion works rides the setting branch's output, the minute hand's axle. The hour wheel is a round-bored 16T clutch gear that idles on that same axle, LEGO's own clock idiom. Four meshes bring the train back to the axle it left: two cannot, because no two catalogue pairs with equal centre distances multiply to 12, and three would turn the hour hand backwards. So in set the crown turns the hands through the gears: 3 crown turns per minute-hand turn, and 36 per hour-hand turn. In wind and neutral the hands stand still, because no going train drives them here. No hand part is placed. The two angles are the reading.

The fusee is a cord on a stepped cone of grooved pulleys, climbing one step a wrap, from a barrel the spring turns. The steps are SOLVED so that torque × radius is as level as a staircase allows. The spring's law is POSED: a LEGO spring (a rubber band, a pull-back motor) can only be measured, and until it is the table holds the steel ribbon's law as a stand-in. Its maintaining power is Harrison's: a click pawl rides the ratchet, a detent holds the ratchet, and a band from it drives the great wheel while the fusee is wound back. Which way each pawl locks is read off the teeth. The bands' forces are posed. The great wheel drives the train's next wheel, the centre pinion: an 8T, five times as fast the other way, turned through the tooth counts. It drives nothing yet.

The fusee runs a loop. Running down, the cord leaves the cone one step at a time and the barrel takes it: the fusee turns at the train's steady rate, so the barrel speeds up when the cord comes down onto the larger step. At run-down a crank winds the fusee back, and the cord goes back on the base, the largest step, first: the wrap that came off last goes on first. At every state the cord leaves each pulley on the tangent, so it runs straight from the cone to the barrel. The click's pawl rides its wheel, the detent holds the ratchet, and the maintaining band keeps the great wheel turning forward, because the train must not stop while the watch is wound. At full wind the going spring first re-tensions the band, and the run starts again. The two bars are the measurement this module exists for: the spring's torque, which falls the whole way down, and the torque after the cone, which the steps hold in a band, each against its value at full wind. Both read a POSED spring law, and the train's rate is a display rate. The loop is modelled, not simulated: every angle comes from the cord paid through the parts' own geometry, and no force is integrated.

The alarm is the movement's alarm as LEGO. Its column wheel is two three-blade rotors, one turned over, so six columns stand 60° apart; a finger presses a pusher, an axle sliding in two Technic bricks, whose tip pushes a half bush on the end of a driver idling on the wheel's axle; the driver's pawl pushes a 12-tooth wheel one tooth, and a banded detent holds each of twelve rests. A press short of the detent's crest falls back; a full press steps the wheel half a column pitch, so on and off alternate. The release is a ring of three pins geared 12:1 off an hour axle, the fourth place empty: no stock disc has a notch, so the notch is built. The release lever rides the pins; where the empty place comes under it, it drops, and its far end lets go of the fly. A pin under the lever reads the column wheel: on a column the lever is held, and the alarm stays silent. The striking train steps the barrel up 4:1 to the fly's arbor, where four pins lift the hammer, which falls onto a brick: no LEGO part rings. The governor is a fly, two beams that the air brakes. It is a different mechanism from the movement's anchor, and it has no period. So the cadence and the strikes per wind are posed until a band is measured, and the bands are drawn posed.

The alarm cell presses itself: a full press, a half press, a full press, a half press, each followed by the hour coming round, so the lever reads the wheel. press, half-press and the stroke slider take over. The pawl first turns with the driver until it meets a tooth's face, then drives the wheel; the detent climbs the tooth to its crest. Let go short of the crest, the wheel falls back with the pawl; past the far crest, the detent drops it into the next rest; between the two it stands on the tooth's tip, where the model does not say which way it falls. At the hour a column under the lever's pin holds the lever, and the alarm stays silent; a gap lets it drop, the fly runs and the hammer strikes. It is modelled, not simulated: every angle is read off the parts, and every speed is a display rate.

The escapement is the movement's oscillator as LEGO, and both of its halves are ANALOGUES. The steel's Swiss lever cannot be built: a LEGO tooth has no locking corner, and a lever's drop is a fraction of a degree, inside the play of a plastic axle in a plastic hole. So this is an anchor, a recoil escapement: two pallets on one rocking body, each in turn catching a tooth of the escape wheel and letting it go. Nothing in LEGO is fine enough to be a pallet but a gear tooth, so each pallet is an 8T on a friction pin, turned to the one angle that works and held there. The oscillator is a balance arm on a knife edge: no LEGO part is spring steel, so there is no hairspring, and gravity restores it. It is a pendulum drawn as a balance: its period comes from its inertia and how far its centre of mass hangs below the knife, and the gears on its arms are chosen so that period is one second. While a pallet holds a tooth and the balance swings on, the wheel is pushed BACK (recoil); as the balance returns, the tooth pushes the pallet (impulse) until it slips off the pallet's tip (unlock) and the wheel runs free to the other pallet (drop, lock). The swing's size is POSED, inside the range the parts allow. It is modelled, not simulated: the wheel's place at every angle of the swing is read off the parts, and no force is weighed.
