## 299. Nothing prices WEAR: declare the service interval and the design life, and hold the escape wheel to 100 years of 5-yearly service

Asked by the owner (2026-11-09), after TODO 296 closed: *"how do we make the
construction more realistic and survive 100 years of wear and tear?"* — then
narrowed to a target: **regular service every 5 years, and the escape wheel
lasting 100 years of it.** The service is the ordinary one: *"replacing
expendable parts and regreasing and lubricating"*, and *"cleaning / removal of
ground up metal dust and other old grease and dust material."*

**The repo prices STRENGTH and has no wear story at all.** Three strength
criteria are in the tree, each with its source: yield (the pivots at their
service load, TODO 192 step 2; the click's arm and beak, TODO 217; the plate's
nickel, §336; both mainspring ribbons, TODO 193), FATIGUE (the hairspring
against `HAIRSPRING_FATIGUE_MPA` = 700, Shigley's S'e = 0.5·Sut — the one
endurance limit in the repo, and it is the member doing 7.9e9 cycles), and since
TODO 296 landing 2 SHAKEDOWN (the escape tooth's tip). Not one of them prices
material REMOVAL. There is no hardness figure for any working pair, no wear
coefficient, no sliding distance, and no cycle count anywhere — TODO 251 noted
the gap in passing ("wear was not measured: no wear law or hardness pair is
declared anywhere to price it with") and left it. Wear, not fracture, is what
ends watches.

**And "100 years" is meaningless without the service.** No watch survives a
century untouched; it survives twenty overhauls. The schedule is what makes a
wear number computable at all, and the owner's three clauses each do specific
work in the model:

- **Relubrication** means the oil is fresh for most of every interval, so the
  wear regime is BOUNDARY-LUBRICATED throughout. Without it the right
  coefficient would be a dry or starved one, orders worse.
- **Cleaning out the ground-up metal and old grease** means the debris is
  removed every interval, so there is no abrasive third body accumulating and
  the wear does not compound across intervals. This is what entitles the model
  to a mild-wear (Archard) law with a constant coefficient rather than a
  runaway one.
- **Replacing expendable parts** splits the movement in two, and that
  classification has to be declared, because it decides what each member's
  criterion even is. A part that is replaced at service needs to last 5 years;
  a part that is not needs to last 100.

So the item is three things: declare the schedule and the split, derive the
cycle counts off the train, and price the escape wheel against them.

---

**What the first measurements say** (scratch probes, 2026-11-09; the figures
below are what the landing has to reproduce from the build rather than from a
script):

**The counts, derived and controlled.** 100 years is 3.1558e9 s and
**15.779e9 beats** at `SPEC.vph` 18,000. Walking the train's own tooth counts
back up from the escape wheel:

| arbor | turns in 100 years | pivot slide (r 35 µm) |
|---|---|---|
| escape | **5.2596e8** | **116 km** |
| fourth | 5.2596e7 | 11.6 km |
| third | 6.5745e6 | 1.45 km |
| centre | 8.766e5 | 0.32 km |

Each of the 15 teeth engages twice per wheel revolution, so **1.0519e9
engagements per tooth**. The control is that the centre arbor must turn once an
hour and the fourth once a minute — facts about a watch, not about the
arithmetic — and they do, exactly. That control earned its place immediately:
the first walk was off by one stage (it landed the driven arbor's count on the
driver) and read the centre arbor at 51,135 turns instead of 876,600.

**The pivots are fine, and the reason is interesting.** Sliding distance rises
600× down the train while the load falls 500×, so the product F·s is nearly
constant (0.104 N·m at the centre against 0.074 at the escape) — because the
stages dissipate nearly equal friction work, which the energy column already
prices. Wear volume is that work over the hardness, so **the train's pivots wear
nearly equally**: 0.35–0.65 µm of depth at the adverse coefficient over the
whole century, against a `PIVOT_BORE_CLEAR` of 18.95 µm. Two to three percent of
the fit. Nothing to do.

**The teeth are the binding site, and it is not close.** The tip's slip across a
pallet stone is **0.509 mm per engagement** (0.157 u down the locking face at the
landing and back up at the unlock, plus 1.028 u along the stone's impulse face),
so **535 km per tooth** over the century — and unlike a pivot, that wear is
concentrated at a wedge tip, so the same volume buys a far larger DEPTH:

| wear coefficient K | tip recession in 100 years |
|---|---|
| 1e-8 (mild, hard-on-hard, clean oil) | 2.3 µm |
| 1e-7 | 7.3 µm |
| 1e-6 (the adverse end of boundary-lubricated steel) | **23.0 µm** |

**The budget is DERIVED, not picked.** Re-reading the escapement's own crossings
at a reduced tooth circle — the stones stay where they were set, only the tooth
shortens — the lock is what goes:

| recession | drop (both stones) | lock + run | impulse |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (as cut) | 1.562° / 1.903° | 0.1573 / 0.1486 u | 10.71° / 10.64° |
| 7.3 µm | 1.514° / 1.748° | 0.1369 / 0.1293 u | 10.47° / 10.25° |
| 15 µm | 1.463° / 1.581° | 0.1154 / 0.1090 u | 10.21° / 9.82° |
| 23 µm | 1.411° / 1.404° | 0.0930 / 0.0878 u | 9.93° / 9.37° |
| 60 µm | 1.180° / 0.534° | 0.0107 / 0.0101 u | 8.57° / 7.08° |

The lock+run of 0.1486 u is `LOCK_DEG` 1.5° plus `RUN_DEG` 0.5°, and the lock is
the part declared against shock ("enough lock that a shock cannot unlock it").
The run is therefore the spendable share: **about 14 µm of recession** takes
lock+run down to the lock alone.

**So the conclusion FLIPS between the corners of the band** — survives at
K ≤ 1e-7, loses its lock at K = 1e-6 — and by `FRICTION`'s own rule a
conclusion that flips between corners is not a conclusion. **The wear
coefficient is the single number that decides whether this escape wheel lasts a
century, and it is the one the model does not have.** That is the finding, and
it is more useful than a verdict would have been: it says exactly which
measurement to go and get.

**One thing to get right in the record, so nobody mis-cites it.** A worn tooth
here *reduces* the drop (1.90° → 1.25° at 30 µm), which is the opposite of the
textbook "worn escape teeth give excessive drop". That rule describes a STEP
worn into the impulse FACE; this is a tip shortened radially, a different wear
mode with the opposite sign on the drop and the same sign on the lock.

**What is owed.**

1. **Declare the schedule and the split.** `SERVICE_INTERVAL_YEARS` (5, the
   owner's, inside the trade's 3–7) and `DESIGN_LIFE_YEARS` (100), with
   `SERVICE_CLASS` naming every member EXPENDABLE (replaced at service: the oil
   and grease, the mainspring ribbons, gaskets) or LIFETIME (everything else),
   because the class is what picks a member's criterion. A member in neither
   class must fail the gate — the roster's completeness is the point, on
   `LUBRICATION`'s precedent.
2. **Derive the counts off the train**, never typed: beats, engagements per
   tooth, turns and sliding distance per arbor, from `SPEC.vph` and `TRAIN`'s
   own tooth counts (standing rule 2 — the counts travel the gears like the
   angles do). With the once-an-hour / once-a-minute control as a boot assert.
3. **Declare the wear law and its coefficient as a BAND with its source**, on
   `FRICTION`'s rule, and read the adverse end: Archard's K for
   boundary-lubricated hardened steel against sapphire. Hardness is DERIVED from
   the yield bands already declared (Tabor's H ≈ 3σ_y), not added as a second
   independent number.
4. **Price the escape wheel both ways** — its teeth against the derived lock
   budget, its pivots against `PIVOT_BORE_CLEAR` — and gate what can be gated.
   Where the band straddles, the row REPORTS and says so; the gate holds the
   arithmetic, the counts, the budget's derivation and the roster, which are the
   parts that do not depend on K.
5. **Then the two sites this item does not claim**: the pallet stones' own faces
   (corundum, four times the hardness and a far larger contact area, so expected
   mild — but unmeasured), and every other `LIFETIME` member's wear. The mesh
   teeth are TODO 251's and TODO 255's ground.

**And the construction changes the answer suggests**, each a separate piece of
work and none of it claimed here:

- **Endstones on the balance.** TODO 254 is open: the balance's lower pivot ends
  at its stone's underside with no endstone and no oil sink. It is the fastest,
  most-loaded bearing in the watch and the one missing its cap.
- **Oil sinks are geometry, and they are the actual 5-year mechanism.** A dished
  reservoir around a jewel hole is what holds the drop against the pivot for
  years rather than months. §247's map names the sinks and carries a waiver for
  the missing one; cutting them is measurable.
- **A harder tooth is the only lever that moves the teeth's answer.** Recession
  goes as √(K·F·s/H), and F is already 0.2 mN and s is fixed by the lift. The
  industry's answer was a harder escape wheel (nickel-phosphorus LIGA, then
  silicon); the traditional one was a finer polish and epilame to keep the oil at
  the tip. Either is a material declaration this model could carry.
