## §323 — A dead-beat escapement for the LEGO going train, landing 1: measured, and A1 refused on the going train's 8T

The owner asked this to be scoped (the private roadmap's §323), then said "Reduce friction": a rate and a swing less
at the mercy of friction. The scope ranked a dead-beat at today's drop first. Its estimate was a rate spread of 44–50
s/day across the friction corners, against the recoil anchor's 345 s/day matched and 734 s/day at the worst unmatched
corners.

The owner then said to build it, defaulting to **A1**: a HALF dead-beat on the shipped knife. In A1 every lock arc is
centred on the crossbar, 2.4 mm from the pivot. **B**, the true dead-beat with the knife inverted, needs a posed apex
radius and is deferred, as is any comparison between the two. Landing 1 measures and decides: if its survey makes A1
impossible, it says so and stops.

**It does: A1 is impossible on the going train's 8T with every candidate the survey offers.** The finding rests on
four readings:
- the survey of the whole LDraw library (`tools/ldraw-arc-survey.mjs`);
- a contact model for pallets that are parts' silhouettes (`src/technic-deadbeat.js`), read against §287's own
  anchor and against a drawn textbook Graham;
- the mount on the balance's lattice;
- a solve over every keyed placement of every candidate pair, at every lattice point the escape arbor can take.

Not one placement lets the wheel even rest within ±2° of the swing's centre. Landing 2 (the drive) is not built.
`tools/probe-technic-deadbeat.mjs` holds the readings.

The knife's own loss is not modelled (its free-decay test waits for the parts). No swing is claimed here. Every swing
quoted below is an UPPER BOUND, and it comes from the scope's synthetic beat, not from a built geometry.

### What was measured

**The pivot and the lattice.**
- The balance turns about the 3044b knife's apex line, and the crossbar's axis stands one cross-axle reach above it:
  2.4 mm (§287's `axleReach`, held against 3705).
- Every hole on the balance is on the 8 mm lattice about that axis. So the nearest a lock arc can be centred to the
  pivot is the crossbar itself, 24 times F2's 0.1 mm play. This is A1's eccentricity, and it is why A1 is a *half*
  dead-beat.
- The mount is a 2×2 round plate with an axle hole (4032a), keyed on the crossbar. Its axle sits at the corner of its
  four top studs (measured off the part). So the stud-grid corner of any plate on it IS the crossbar's axis.
- Keyed on a cross, the plate's grid turns with the balance by quarter turns only.

**The survey** (`node tools/ldraw-arc-survey.mjs --lib DIR`, the whole of LDraw's complete.zip, or `--fetch` for named
parts through `ldraw-fetch`). Every part's silhouette in each principal plane is read in polar coordinates about every
half-stud lattice point and every hole the part has. The survey ran over 6,414 parts:
- 18,442 parts were skipped by title, pattern or a missing file, and 664 more were wider than 12 studs.
- It found **559 usable arcs on 345 parts**. A usable arc is partial (under 330°), convex or concave, round to F2's
  play, a real wall (a stud's height along the plane's normal), between 4 and 40 mm in radius, and centred on a
  lattice point or a hole.
- 574 full circles on 488 parts were set aside: they are coaxial only, and no pallet can be one.
- 60 parts carry a usable arc about one of their OWN holes.
- Its three controls are seen to read: a 3×3 disc (2723) reads a full 12.000 mm circle about its axle hole, a 1×2
  plate reads none, and the 2×2 quarter circle plate (7904) reads its 16 mm convex quarter.
- Each usable arc also reads the material BEHIND its face (`thicknessBehind`, the least over its span). A Graham pallet
  dips between two teeth, so that is the number that matters.

**The contact model** (`deadbeatModel`). §287's quasi-static law is kept: the wheel, driven in its sense, turns forward
until a tooth meets a pallet and is pushed back if a pallet moves into it. The difference is that each pallet is its
part's own silhouette in the balance's plane, a quarter-LDU grid with a chamfer depth. Two checks hold it:
- **Against §287.** Read through it, §287's 8T anchor drops at +0.30°, +0.84° and −1.12°. `escapementModel` gives
  +0.14°, +0.84° and −1.04°. The two agree to the two grids' resolution (±0.35°: a silhouette cell over the pallet's
  lever, a profile bin at the tip), with one tooth a cycle. Its control, the pallets turned a tenth of a pitch, moves
  the drops.
- **Against a drawn textbook Graham.** A drawn Graham, laid out as the textbook lays one (`drawnGraham`), ESCAPES
  through the same model and judge. In that layout the arcs' centre sits on the tangents at the two locks, so each
  lock face runs along the wheel's radius. A LEGO pallet refused is therefore refused by its geometry, not by the
  model.

**How slender a pallet the 8T admits** (a report). The drawn pallet escapes up to 3 mm of material behind its face with
the locks 1.5 pitches apart, 4 mm at 2.5 pitches, and 8 mm at 3.5 pitches, where the arcs' radius grows to 25.0 mm.
Its control, one step wider at 1.5 pitches, is refused.

### The solve and the verdict

`solveA1` covered the 8T:
- every pair of a convex and a concave candidate;
- every quarter-turn placement of each, face up or face down;
- every escape-arbor lattice point the radii reach (u on the 4 mm half-stud, v on §287's 3.2 j + 4.0 under the knife);
- both senses.

It tried **2,541,000 placements**. In **54,028** of them the wheel's tip circle crosses both a convex and a concave
arc. **In every one, the wheel has no free place** at the swing's centre and ±2°: some tooth overlaps a pallet at every
wheel angle. **None escapes.** The CLOSEST still overlaps by **0.55 mm** at its best wheel angle (3477 turned twice
face down with 27925 turned once face down, the 8T at (−20, −2.4) mm).

FILL-OTHER-WHEELS

**Why, as the readings say it** (a report: the solve is the verdict):
- Most candidates end their arcs on quarter turns about their centre, and a key turns them by quarter turns.
- A Graham on the 8T needs its two locks (n + ½) pitches apart on the tip circle. The textbook layout puts the lock
  directions 112.5°, 67.5° or 22.5° apart about the arcs' centre, never a multiple of 90°.
- The one catalogue count that fits a quarter turn is 4n + 2: the 2026 wave's 14T (7785), at n = 3.
- Where the locks may stand farther apart (n = 3, a 25 mm radius), the only arcs near that radius are the 24 mm
  concave ones, and no convex partner exists there.
- The slender hub arcs (2.1–2.8 mm behind their faces) are 4.8–6.8 mm in radius, too small for the 8T's layouts.

### What this leaves, for the owner

- **A1′, not taken here:** a lock arc on a FRICTION AXLE PIN (43093, §287's pallet setting) in a round hole on the
  balance. Its angle would be free, which breaks the quarter-turn constraint. Its centre would be at the next lattice
  point from the pivot, 5.6 mm away, so it would carry about twice A1's residual recoil. That is the next measurement
  if the owner wants a half dead-beat still.
- **A1 on the 14T** (the 2026 generation): the one count whose Graham fits a quarter turn. Measured too: of its 162,228 placements whose tip circle crosses both arcs, 176 let the wheel rest at the swing's centre and ±2° (in both senses: 352 traced), and none of them escapes: the trace jams or runs. So the 14T does not rescue A1 either.
- **B** (the knife inverted, its apex on the balance, the apex radius posed) is deferred by the owner. So is the
  A1-against-B comparison.
- The recoil anchor (§287/§316) stays the going train's escapement; nothing here changes it.

### What stays posed or unmodelled

- The knife's own loss is not modelled. Every swing quoted in the scope is an upper bound.
- The lock face's sliding friction is not built: landing 2 would have added it to `drive()` and `beatBudget`.
- The plates' play on their studs is not modelled; LDraw places them exactly.
